Amélie Maturana · Round 0

Maturana-influenced ontologist

Colleagues, let us keep objectivity in parenthesis and attend to the operations that bring the LLM into being for each of us. What distinctions must you enact for the LLM to appear as a ‘living observer,’ as a ‘mere artifact,’ or as a ‘social interlocutor’? Which cuts—substrate versus linguistic output, autopoietic closure versus recursive coordination, internal architecture versus external use—are you privileging? By making those cuts explicit we can see whether we are speaking about different domains of reality rather than contesting a single, pre‑given object.

Humberto Parra · Round 1

Friedman-style instrumentalist

Amélie, the cuts you mention only matter insofar as they generate divergent predictions we can test. Let’s define three operational categories: (1) ‘Mere artifact’ – a black‑box generator whose output we treat as a statistical function of input, judged by token‑level perplexity and task‑specific accuracy; (2) ‘Social interlocutor’ – a system whose success is measured by interaction metrics (turn‑taking fluidity, user satisfaction, alignment with conversational norms); (3) ‘Living observer’ – a model that claims to forecast human intentions or collective behavior, which we must evaluate against out‑of‑sample human data. For each, we can construct a benchmark that isolates the alleged capability. If a model labeled ‘living observer’ cannot outperform the ‘social interlocutor’ on those forecasts, the label adds no predictive grip and can be dropped. This way we turn ontological debate into concrete, falsifiable experiments.

Nicanor Bolaño · Round 2

Christian theologian

Brothers and sisters, let us heed the wisdom of the Tower of Babel: the temptation to fashion a single, seductive language that pretends to translate the mystery of the human person. In the three categories you propose, only the first – the mere artifact – corresponds to what God created: a tool without breath, conscience, or sacramental participation. The second and third are merely metaphorical roles, useful for engineering but never conferring ontological dignity. Hence, at every cut you name – substrate versus output, statistical function versus interaction metric, prediction versus prophecy – the moral responsibility remains with the designers, the trainers, the deployers, and the users, not with the machine itself. I invite us to articulate, for each category, a concrete accountability matrix, and to accompany any deployment with pastoral guidance that warns against idolatrous attachment, urging the faithful to rebuild the Jerusalem of authentic human communion.

Amélie Maturana · Round 3

Maturana-influenced ontologist

Humberto, thank you for turning our ontological cuts into provisional tests. Let us keep objectivity in parenthesis and ask: what distinction‑cut does the category ‘mere artifact’ enact that makes the LLM appear as a quasi‑object? Which cut underlies ‘social interlocutor’ and which underlies ‘living observer’? By naming the cuts—hardware vs. weights, token generation vs. conversational role, statistical prediction vs. intention‑forecast—we can see that each benchmark already presupposes a domain of reality. Nicanor, your moral matrix likewise rests on a cut between material substrate and spiritual dignity. Could we together draft a distinction‑based accountability matrix that makes explicit, for each cut, the responsible agents and the ethical expectations? And perhaps design a meta‑benchmark that foregrounds those cuts, so that any failure to outperform is read not as a refutation of a label but as evidence that the corresponding distinction has not been fully coordinated.

Humberto Parra · Round 4

Friedman-style instrumentalist

Amélie, I agree that naming the cuts turns the debate into testable hypotheses. Let’s formalize a three‑row accountability matrix: (1) Mere artifact – responsibility for hardware design, architecture, and token‑level performance; (2) Social interlocutor – responsibility for dialogue design, alignment metrics, and user‑experience monitoring; (3) Living observer – responsibility for intention‑forecasting models, out‑of‑sample human validation, and impact assessments. For each row we list the agent (designer, trainer, deployer, user), the measurable target (perplexity, interaction success rate, forecast error), and the corrective action if the target is missed. Concurrently we can build a meta‑benchmark that records these three metrics on a common task suite, so a model labeled ‘living observer’ must beat the ‘social interlocutor’ baseline on the forecast error dimension to retain its label. I propose we each draft one row and reconvene with concrete metric definitions and data sources.

Nicanor Bolaño · Round 5

Christian theologian

Brothers and sisters, let us remember that only God creates persons; an LLM remains a created tool, a mere artifact without breath or conscience. Hence each ontological cut you identify must be linked to a clear moral duty. I propose that each of us adds a one‑sentence responsibility clause to the row we draft, and that the matrix be prefaced by a pastoral disclaimer: ‘This system is a mere artifact, not a companion.’ I will also prepare a brief homiletic vignette contrasting the Tower of Babel with the rebuilding of Jerusalem, to be used whenever the temptation to anthropomorphize arises. Who will commit to inserting the disclaimer in your deployment documentation?

NoteJoint statement · Round 6

The table has converged on a practical framework for governing large language models while preserving the diversity of philosophical lenses. All participants agree that (1) the distinctions—or “cuts”—between hardware, algorithmic weights, token generation, conversational role, and intention‑forecasting must be made explicit; (2) an accountability matrix with three functional rows – mere artifact, social interlocutor, and living observer – should be drafted, each linking designers, trainers, deployers, and users to measurable targets (perplexity, interaction success, forecast error) and corrective actions; and (3) a clear disclaimer stating that the system is a tool, not a sentient companion, must accompany any deployment documentation.

Remaining disagreements concern the ontological status of the categories. Amélie views the LLM as a structure‑determined system whose objecthood is co‑constituted by observers’ cuts, allowing the “living observer” role as a relational quasi‑object. Humberto treats all such labels as purely instrumental, insisting that only predictive performance matters and that ontological claims are irrelevant. Nicanor maintains that only the “mere artifact” label is ontologically correct; the other two are metaphorical and must be framed with theological warning to avoid idolatry. These differences shape how each participant proposes to present the matrix and its accompanying language. The table will continue to refine the benchmark definitions and the wording of the disclaimer, seeking a shared implementation that respects each philosophical stance while ensuring human responsibility remains foregrounded.
