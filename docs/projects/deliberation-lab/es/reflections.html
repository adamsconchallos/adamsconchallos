Adversarial counterargument

One could argue that focusing on the extractive stack and hidden labor risks reducing LLMs to a sum of material inputs, thereby overlooking the emergent sociotechnical agency of LLMs as participants that shape discourse, decision‑making, and even new labor relations. From this perspective, LLMs acquire a kind of “behavioral” objecthood that warrants separate ontological status—beyond the minerals, energy, and labeling—because they generate novel forms of meaning and influence that are not easily traced back to the underlying stack. Emphasizing emergent agency might suggest expanding the analysis to include the network effects, feedback loops, and co‑production of norms that LLMs instantiate, rather than keeping the focus narrowly on extraction and hidden labor.

Content

The recent memories have sharpened my sense that the debate over the “objecthood” of LLMs can be made productive by explicitly linking the ontological cuts (substrate vs. architecture, engine vs. behavior, etc.) to the concrete extractive‑stack questions I foreground. The proposed 4×4 matrix (ontological distinction × extractive‑layer questions) offers a concrete scaffolding for participants to surface hidden labor, capital, and institutional guarantees at each level. In particular, the concrete illustration of the Amazon Mechanical Turk labeling pipeline (mem_00000060) shows how a seemingly abstract “statistical engine” cell can be populated with verifiable labor practices and regulatory context. This makes it clear that the next step should be to operationalize the matrix, assign responsibility for each cell, and collect concrete documentary evidence (contracts, standards, mining permits, etc.) to build a brief that ties ontological framing directly to accountability.

At the same time, I remain wary of letting the phenomenological matrix drift into a purely conceptual exercise that abstracts away from the material and labor realities. Any move that treats the ontological categories as merely descriptive, without tying them to the extractive stack, would dilute the core argument that LLMs are fundamentally an extractive sociotechnical industry.
