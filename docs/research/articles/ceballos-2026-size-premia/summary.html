Is a bigger salmon worth more?

What Chilean fresh salmon export prices show

A policy summary of Size premia in export prices: Evidence from Chilean salmon exports, by Hans-Martin Straume and Adams Ceballos-Concha, accepted for publication in Aquaculture Economics & Management.

The question

Chile is the world’s second-largest salmon producer, after Norway. What are these salmon like, and what prices do they fetch? One might think that a bigger salmon fetches a higher price. However, assuming that buyers only think in kilos is naive, to say the least. Buyers look for pieces that fit their cuts, their portions, and their customers.

Understanding this matters for producers and exporters. The weight at which salmon is harvested is a production decision. If price changes with size, that decision has economic consequences. It also matters for those who design export and value-added policies.

What we did

We use customs records of Chilean exports of fresh whole salmon and fresh fillets between 2012 and 2025. That is more than 900,000 shipments!

With these data we compare very similar shipments. They are shipments by the same exporter, to the same country, in the same month, that differ in fish size. So the price difference does not reflect that a firm charges more in general, or that the market rose that month. We analyze whole salmon and fillets separately. We also check whether the differences change with the time of year, the destination country, and the scale of the exporter.

What we found

For whole salmon, size changes the average price very little

Average price differences across sizes of whole salmon are small. Fish of 2 to 3 kg sell at a discount of about 1.3% relative to fish of 5 to 6 kg. Fish above 6 kg earn a premium of close to 1.5%. For fish above 7 kg, that premium falls below 1% once shipment size and transport mode are taken into account.

For fillets, size matters more

For fillets, the relationship between size and price is stronger. There are clear discounts and premiums by size. Fillets of 0.5 to 1 kg sell at a discount of about 7% relative to fillets of 1 to 1.5 kg. Fillets of 1.5 to 2 kg earn a premium of about 2.5%. Fillets above 2 kg earn a smaller premium, close to 1%. In other words, the largest fillets are not the best paid. The highest value lies in the intermediate sizes.

Filleting is associated with more stable prices over the year

Do these differences change with the time of year? For whole salmon there is some seasonal variation, although the evidence is limited. It is concentrated in the smallest fish, of 2 to 3 kg, and in the largest, above 7 kg. For fillets we find no seasonal pattern. Small fillets carry a discount all year, and fillets of 1.5 to 2 kg a premium all year. One possible explanation is that filleting turns fish of variable sizes into more standardized products. That would give a more stable price structure over the year.

Each market values sizes differently

Destinations are highly concentrated. Brazil receives about 62% of the export value of fresh whole salmon. The United States receives 94% of the value of fresh fillets.

The value of a size also depends on where it is sold. Differences across markets are significant for both whole salmon and fillets. Markets also receive different sizes. China accounts for a larger share of large whole salmon and pays a premium of about 5% for them. In contrast, the United States pays about 5% less for fish of 2 to 3 kg. Argentina has its largest share among small fish, of 2 to 3 kg.

Who exports also matters

Exports are concentrated in a few firms. The quarter of exporters with the highest export value in each month accounts for about two thirds of total value. Size premiums differ with exporter scale, for both whole salmon and fillets. For whole salmon, smaller exporters tend to obtain lower premiums for large fish. But the largest exporters do not obtain better relative prices for every size. This suggests that buyer networks and each firm’s processing capacity also play a role.

What this means for policy and industry

What can decision makers in government or industry do with these results? We propose four takeaways.

  1. A bigger salmon does not guarantee a better price. Decisions on when to harvest and at what weight should weigh the product, the destination market, and the time of year.
  2. Each size has its market. Directing each size to the market that values it most can improve the price obtained. This information can be useful for export promotion and market access.
  3. Processing in Chile plays a role. Filleting is associated with more stable relative prices over the year.
  4. Commercial relationships count. Exporter characteristics and links with buyers appear to influence the price of each size.

Reference
